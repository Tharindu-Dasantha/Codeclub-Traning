## 📘 Detailed Module Breakdown

### 🧠 Module 1: What is AI, Really? (00:00 - 00:15)

#### Objective:
Demystify AI before using it — students should see it as a powerful but limited tool, not magic.

#### Facilitator Script & Steps:
1. **The Hook — "The World's Best Guesser":**
   - Ask: *"When your phone suggests the next word while you're typing a message, is it 'thinking'? Or is it guessing based on patterns from millions of messages it's seen before?"*
   - Reveal: Generative AI is like a supercharged version of that autocomplete — trained on huge amounts of text, images, and code, it predicts what a good next word, pixel, or line of code probably looks like, based on patterns. It does **not** understand meaning the way a person does, and it does **not** know if something is actually true — it just knows what's *likely*.
2. **What AI is GOOD at:**
   - Generating first drafts quickly (images, text, code snippets).
   - Combining ideas in creative, sometimes surprising ways.
   - Explaining a coding concept in a different way if you're stuck.
3. **What AI is NOT good at (yet):**
   - Guaranteeing something is 100% factually correct (it can confidently state wrong things — this is called a **hallucination**).
   - Truly understanding your specific project or intentions the way you do.
   - Being a substitute for you learning and understanding your own code.
4. **The Golden Rule for This Whole Session:** *"AI is your assistant. You are still the author. If you don't understand what it gave you, don't use it yet — ask it to explain, or ask a trusted adult/facilitator."*

### 🎨 Module 2: AI for Creativity — Prompting for Images (00:15 - 00:40)

#### Objective:
Teach students how to write an effective prompt for an AI image generator, and how to iterate.

#### Key Concept: The Prompt Formula 🖌️
A great image prompt usually includes:
```text
[SUBJECT] + [STYLE] + [SETTING] + [MOOD/DETAILS]

Example:
"A friendly elephant (SUBJECT), in a colorful cartoon illustration style (STYLE),
walking through a Sri Lankan tea plantation at sunrise (SETTING),
warm golden light, joyful mood (MOOD/DETAILS)"
```
- **Weak prompt:** `"elephant"` → vague, generic result.
- **Strong prompt:** `"A friendly cartoon elephant walking through a Sri Lankan tea plantation at sunrise, warm golden light, joyful mood"` → far more specific and useful result.

#### Live Demo Activity (Facilitator-Run, Projected):
1. Using the **one supervised, adult-controlled account** set up per the README's safety guidance, project the AI image tool on the main screen.
2. Try a weak, vague prompt first as a class — observe the generic result.
3. As a class, rewrite it together using the Subject + Style + Setting + Mood formula, and regenerate. Compare!
4. Take 2-3 more prompt suggestions called out by students (facilitator can lightly edit for appropriateness before running), and generate them live. Discuss what worked and what didn't.
5. **Key teaching moment — Iteration:** AI image generation is rarely perfect on the first try. Real creative work with AI means **refining your prompt** based on what you see, just like redrafting a piece of writing.

#### Guided Practice:
Students individually (on paper, in `ACTIVITIES.md` Activity 1) write 2 prompts of their own using the formula, to be tried later or as homework/facilitator follow-up.

### ⚖️ Module 3: AI Ethics & Responsible Use (00:40 - 01:05)

#### Objective:
Build a working understanding of the key ethical considerations before students use AI hands-on for coding.

#### 1. Hallucination — AI Can Be Confidently Wrong 🚩
- AI sometimes states incorrect facts with total confidence, because it's predicting what *sounds* right, not checking a database of truth.
- **Rule:** Always verify important facts from AI with a trusted, reliable source (a teacher, a reputable website, a book) — especially for schoolwork.

#### 2. Bias — AI Learns from Imperfect Data 🌍
- AI is trained on huge amounts of real-world text and images, which contain real-world biases and imbalances (e.g., it might default to unfamiliar or stereotypical results for certain cultures, names, or professions if not prompted carefully).
- **Rule:** Notice when a result feels one-sided, stereotypical, or "off," and don't assume the AI's first answer is neutral or complete.

#### 3. Privacy — Never Overshare with an AI Chatbot 🔒
- This connects directly to Golden Rule 1 from the Online Safety Workshop! Many AI chat tools store conversations, and some may use them to improve future versions.
- **Rule:** Never type your full name + school, home address, phone number, or passwords into an AI chatbot — the same rules as any other online tool.

#### 4. Authorship & Originality — You're Still the Author ✍️
- If AI generates an image or code for you, is it fully "yours"? This is a genuinely debated question even among adults! For this workshop: **using AI as a tool is fine, but claiming AI-generated work as 100% your own unaided creation, without understanding or being able to explain it, is not honest.**
- **Rule:** Be able to explain any code or work you submit, whether or not AI helped write it. If you can't explain it, you don't understand it yet — go back and learn it first.

#### Guided Discussion:
Ask the class: *"If an AI writes a whole website for you and you don't understand any of the code, did you build a website?"* Let students debate — there's no single "gotcha" answer, but the discussion builds judgment.

### ☕ Energy Break & Quiz (01:05 - 01:15)

- **Physical Energizer — "Fact or AI Fiction?" (3 mins):**
  - Facilitator reads a statement (some true, some deliberately wrong-but-confident-sounding, "hallucination-style").
  - If **True**, students give a thumbs up. If **False/Hallucination**, students wave both hands like "nope!"
- **5-Question Rapid Fire Quiz:**
  1. What is it called when AI confidently states something false? (*Hallucination*)
  2. Name one thing you should never type into an AI chatbot. (*Personal info — address, phone number, passwords, etc.*)
  3. What are the 4 parts of a strong image prompt? (*Subject, Style, Setting, Mood/Details*)
  4. True or False: AI results can reflect bias from the data they were trained on. (True!)
  5. If AI writes code for you, whose job is it to understand and check it? (*Yours! You're still the author.*)

### 💻 Module 4: Vibe Coding — Upgrade Your Website (01:15 - 01:45)

#### Objective:
Apply the "describe it, generate it, test it, understand it" loop to make real, working improvements to each student's own website.

#### What is "Vibe Coding"?
Describing what you want in plain English to an AI coding assistant, letting it suggest code, and then **reading, testing, and adjusting** that code yourself — rather than writing every line by hand, but also never blindly trusting it untested.

#### The Vibe Coding Loop 🔁
```text
1. DESCRIBE  → Tell the AI exactly what you want, in plain English.
2. GENERATE  → Let the AI suggest code.
3. TEST      → Paste it in, save, and check it in the browser. Does it actually work?
4. UNDERSTAND → Can you explain what the new code does, in your own words?
5. FIX/ITERATE → If something's broken or you don't understand it, describe the
                 problem back to the AI, or adjust it yourself, and test again.
```

#### Live Demo Activity (Facilitator-Run, Projected):
1. Open a sample website's `style.css` and describe a change out loud, e.g.: *"Make the card hover effect lift up more and add a colorful glowing shadow."*
2. Run the prompt on the supervised AI coding assistant tool. Read the suggested CSS together as a class **before** pasting it in — ask: *"Does this look like real CSS syntax we've learned? Any properties we don't recognize? Let's look them up together."*
3. Paste it in, save, refresh the browser — did it work? If not, describe the problem back to the AI and try again.

#### Main Project — "Phase 2 Website Upgrade"
Students open their own HTML/CSS website (or the fallback baseline) and, using facilitator-run or approved-access AI tools, make **at least 2 AI-assisted improvements**, following the checklist in `ACTIVITIES.md` Activity 2:
- [x] Write a clear, specific prompt describing a real improvement (not "make it better" — be specific!).
- [x] Get an AI-suggested code snippet.
- [x] Test it in the browser.
- [x] Be able to explain, in their own words, what the new code does.
- [x] Note anything they had to fix, reject, or didn't understand (and ask about it!).

### 🌟 Module 5: Showcase & Wrap-up (01:45 - 02:00)

1. **Peer Gallery Walk (5 mins):**
   - Students show a neighbor their "Phase 2" website improvement and explain, in their own words, what the AI-suggested code does.
2. **Design Awards (3 mins):**
   - Award fun badges/stickers:
     - 🎨 *Best Prompt Award* (Most creative, well-structured image prompt)
     - 💻 *Vibe Coding Award* (Best-explained AI-assisted website improvement)
     - ⚖️ *AI Ethics Award* (Sharpest observation during the ethics discussion)
3. **Closing Recap & What's Next (2 mins):**
   - Recap: AI is a powerful creative and coding assistant — but you are always the author, the fact-checker, and the final decision-maker.
   - Teaser: *"You've now built, styled, added logic to, connected to real sensors, and AI-enhanced a project. What will you build next?"*
4. **Certificate / Sticker Handout.**

## 👩‍🏫 Facilitator Tips & Troubleshooting

- **If a student's AI-suggested code doesn't work:** Use it as a teaching moment, not a failure — this is exactly why the "TEST" and "UNDERSTAND" steps exist. Debug it together like any other code.
- **If students ask to use AI on their own personal accounts/devices at home:** Remind them (and consider sending a note to parents) about the age-appropriate account guidance in `README.md`, and that a trusted adult should be aware of and involved in setting up any account.
- **If the ethics discussion in Module 3 runs long:** That's a good sign of engagement! Trim Module 2's live demo iterations slightly rather than rushing the ethics discussion.
- **Keep the tone empowering, not fearful:** The goal is confident, critical, responsible use of a genuinely useful tool — not avoidance of AI altogether.
